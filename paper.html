Selling School: What U.S. Private School Websites Signal About Identity, Rigor, and Price

Prashant Garg  ·  [Coauthor]  ·  2026
Abstract

Fewer than half of U.S. private schools post tuition information on their websites, yet those that do grow 4 percentage points faster per year. We exploit this variation to study how school website content — the primary signal available to families choosing among private schools — predicts enrollment growth and closure in a market where mandatory quality disclosure is limited.

We crawl 14,384 private school websites (40.5% of the PSS 2021–22 universe) and score each school on six focal dimensions using a structured regex ontology: religious identity, character and values, family partnership, college-preparatory rigor, tuition transparency, and STEM emphasis. Tuition transparency is the strongest enrollment predictor: +4.0 percentage points per standard deviation (p < 0.001), concentrated in elementary schools. College-prep emphasis adds +2.8 pp (p < 0.001). In more competitive local markets, schools shift away from religious emphasis (partial r = −0.083***) and toward STEM (r = +0.080***), consistent with religious schools serving bounded denominational markets. County Republican vote share is positively correlated with religious emphasis (r = +0.072***) and negatively correlated with every secular topic.

Website content is not epiphenomenal: it encodes strategic positioning decisions that predict real market outcomes in a sector where public quality signals are thin.

Results in detail

Enrollment growth — tuition transparency and college prep

We estimate OLS regressions of log-enrollment growth on topic scores, controlling for state fixed effects, log enrollment, PSS affiliation type, and school-level covariates. Standard errors are clustered by school ID.

Tuition transparency enters with β = +0.040 (SE = 0.004, p < 0.001) — about 4 percentage points of annual enrollment growth per SD. At the 75th percentile of transparency scores relative to the 25th, this implies roughly 3.2 pp faster growth. College prep enters with β = +0.028 (SE = 0.003, p < 0.001).

Binary and tercile representations are directionally identical and nearly as large. Dropping California, adding year × state interactions, or restricting to nonsectarian schools does not materially change the coefficients.

Closure risk — tuition transparency and grade span

We estimate logit models of school closure on topic scores using a six-state panel (CA, IL, NY, OH, PA, WI; 1,325 school-years, 70 closure events). Standard errors are clustered by school.

Tuition transparency enters the closure logit with a positive coefficient (+0.277, SE = 0.163, p = 0.091), suggesting that transparent schools are more likely to close — consistent with financially stressed schools posting prices as a last-resort effort to attract families. No other topic significantly predicts closure in the joint specification.

Grade-span heterogeneity: the closure effect is largest for K–12 combined schools (OR = +0.850**). Secondary-school closure cells have only 8 events; those estimates are imprecise and should be treated with caution.

Competition and local market structure

We construct local competitor counts using a k-d tree on PSS coordinates, counting same-sector private schools within 10 km. We compute partial Pearson correlations of topic scores with log competitor count, controlling for state fixed effects and log enrollment (N ≈ 14,000 private crawled schools with coordinates).

Religious emphasis shows the strongest negative association (r = −0.083***): schools in denser markets moderate religious signaling. STEM shows the strongest positive association (r = +0.080***): schools in denser markets signal STEM more, consistent with STEM programs drawing from broader geographic markets. Character (r = +0.058***) and college prep (r = +0.043***) also rise with competition.

Geographic and political-geographic gradients

Using 2020 county presidential vote data (MIT Election Data and Science Lab) merged via county FIPS codes, we compute partial correlations of topic scores with county Republican vote share, controlling for state FE and log enrollment.

Religious presence is the only topic positively correlated with Republican vote share (r = +0.072***). Tuition transparency (r = −0.088***), STEM (r = −0.074***), and college prep (r = −0.068***) all run in the opposite direction. The gradient is consistent with political and cultural sorting across counties rather than causal effects of political environment on school strategy.

Social media, SEDA achievement, page placement, and grade span

Social media: LinkedIn presence carries the strongest positive signal across topics (character r = +0.239***; college prep r = +0.168***). TikTok presence is negatively correlated with religious emphasis (r = −0.015*). Additional platform count does not predict enrollment growth once topic scores are controlled.

SEDA achievement: Among public schools, family-partnership emphasis is the strongest predictor of school-level test scores (r = +0.056***), followed by STEM (r = +0.047***). Among private schools, only tuition transparency (r = +0.040***) and STEM (r = +0.036***) significantly associate with county-level achievement.

Page placement: Schools with a dedicated tuition page score 117.5 points higher on transparency than those without — a 2-SD premium, confirming that structural page presence is a powerful proxy for topic emphasis.

Grade span: The tuition-transparency enrollment effect concentrates in elementary schools (β = +0.041***, vs. +0.019 for secondary). The pattern is consistent with elementary-school families facing a longer tuition horizon and placing greater weight on price predictability.
